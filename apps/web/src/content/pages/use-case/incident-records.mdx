## "Walk me through your last incident"

That is the fieldwork request. Not "do you have a status page", which the auditor has already checked, but a sampled incident from the observation period and five questions about it: when did you know, how bad did you decide it was, who was in charge, what did you do and when, and what changed afterwards.

Most teams answer from memory, a Slack thread search and a ticket with a title and a close date. The record is reconstructed weeks later, and the auditor can tell.

This page is about the internal incident record: declaration, severity, commander, timeline, postmortem. The public half, telling customers through a status page (SOC 2 CC2.3, NIS2 Article 23(1), DORA Article 19(3)), is on [status pages for compliance](/use-case/compliance). The requirements themselves are in the [compliance guides](/guides/category/compliance).
</Aside>

## What the record holds

An incident in openstatus is declared by a person, from Slack, the dashboard, the API or an agent. From that moment the record writes itself:

- **Two timestamps, not one.** When the impact started, pre-filled from the monitor when you declare it from the downtime row, and when someone declared it. The gap is visible.
- **A severity decision.** Critical, major or minor, chosen at declaration and changeable later, with every change on the timeline. Canceling an incident records that you looked and decided it was not one.
- **A commander.** One named member in charge, changeable until close, with the handover on the timeline.
- **An append-only timeline.** Status changes with notes, pinned Slack messages with a link back, status report links, postmortem drafted and approved, closed. Each entry carries the time and the member behind it.
- **An approved postmortem.** Summary, impact, timeline, root cause, what went well, what went wrong, action items. Drafted by the agent, corrected by you, approved by an owner, admin or the commander.
- **A frozen close.** Approve the postmortem and close the incident. After that, status, severity, commander and notes cannot change.

## Which control each field answers

<Grid>
<div>

**SOC 2**

CC7.3 asks you to evaluate events and decide whether they are incidents: the declaration and the severity, both timestamped. CC7.4 asks you to respond by executing a defined program: the commander, the status changes and the notes. CC7.5 asks you to restore and communicate resolution: the resolved timestamp, the linked status report and the postmortem action items.

[SOC 2 guide](/guides/soc-2-status-page-requirements)

</div>
<div>

**ISO 27001**

A.5.25, assessment and decision, is the severity and the declared time. A.5.26, response, is the timeline. A.5.27, learning from incidents, is the approved postmortem with owners on the action items. A.5.28, collection of evidence, is the append-only timeline, the frozen record and the audit log.

[ISO 27001 guide](/guides/iso-27001-incident-communication)

</div>
<div>

**NIS2**

The Article 23(4) cascade runs from when you became aware: an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours, and a final report within one month of that notification. The record holds the awareness time, the severity and impact assessment, the mitigation steps as they happened and the root cause for the final report. The filing itself goes through your CSIRT.

[NIS2 guide](/guides/nis2-incident-reporting-requirements)

</div>
<div>

**DORA**

The 4-hour clock starts at classification, and the 24-hour outer limit at awareness. The timeline shows both moments. Duration and service downtime, one of the classification criteria, comes from the monitor incident the record was declared from. The intermediate and final reports draw on the postmortem. Classification is your call.

[DORA guide](/guides/dora-incident-reporting-requirements)

</div>
</Grid>

## Where it stops

Openstatus is the record, not the procedure. It does not write your incident response policy, decide your severity thresholds, classify a DORA major incident, file a NIS2 notification or replace a GRC platform. It makes the evidence those things need exist without anyone reconstructing it afterwards.

## Run one incident this way

1. Declare it from the monitor's downtime row, so the impact start is already filled in. Or declare it from Slack with a title and a severity.
2. Name a commander.
3. Work it where the team already is. If that is the incident's Slack channel, pin the decisions and the rollback messages so they land on the timeline with their author. Otherwise add them as notes.
4. Link the status report your customers read, and keep its wording separate.
5. Mark it mitigated, then resolved, with a note each time.
6. Let the agent draft the postmortem. Correct it, assign the action items, approve it, and close the incident. The record freezes.

The next time an auditor says "walk me through your last incident", open the record.

<Aside type="caution" title="Retention has to cover the observation period">
A SOC 2 Type II window runs 3 to 12 months, and an ISO 27001 cycle is three years. The incident record and its postmortem stay with the workspace. The monitoring data the record was declared from is kept 14 days on Hobby, 3 months on Starter, 12 months on Pro and 24 months on Scale, so pick a plan whose retention spans the period you will be asked about.
</Aside>
