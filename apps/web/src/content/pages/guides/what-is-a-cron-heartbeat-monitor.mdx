Most monitoring answers one question: "is this thing responding?" You call an endpoint, it answers, you check the answer. That works for APIs and websites. It does nothing for the jobs that never answer anyone - the nightly backup, the hourly billing run, the sync that keeps two databases in step.

Those jobs have a nastier failure mode than an outage: they just stop. No error, no log line, no alert. You find out three weeks later when someone needs the backup.

A heartbeat monitor - also called a cron monitor or a dead man's switch - is built for exactly that. Here's how it works and how to set one up so it pages you for real problems and nothing else.

## How a Heartbeat Monitor Works

The direction is flipped compared to an [uptime monitor](/guides/what-is-uptime-monitoring). The monitor never calls your job. Your job calls the monitor.

1. You create a heartbeat monitor and tell it how often your job runs (the **period**) and how late it may be (the **grace period**).
2. The monitor gives you a unique **ping URL**.
3. At the end of each successful run, your job sends a request to that URL.
4. Each ping pushes the deadline forward to `last ping + period + grace`.
5. If the deadline passes without a ping, the monitor goes down and alerts you. The next ping recovers it.

The clock starts with the first ping: a new monitor stays quiet until it has heard from the job once, so you can create it before the job is deployed. That also means a job that never runs at all won't alert - send one ping by hand once it's live to arm the monitor.

```
 ✓ ping      ✓ ping      ✓ ping                 ✗ alert
 ├─ period ──┼─ period ──┼─ period ──┼─ grace ──┤
                                     expected   deadline
```

The signal is the *absence* of a ping. That is the whole trick: it catches failures that produce no output at all.

## Why Error Alerting Isn't Enough

The obvious alternative is "just alert when the job throws". That catches one class of failure - the job ran and broke. It misses every case where the job never ran:

- **The crontab was overwritten** by a config management run or a server rebuild.
- **The host is gone.** The VM was replaced, the container was rescheduled, the old box was decommissioned with the job still on it.
- **The scheduler is down** or paused - a stuck Kubernetes `CronJob`, a disabled CI schedule, a queue consumer that crashed and didn't restart.
- **The job hangs** forever on a lock or a network call and never reaches the line that would have logged an error.

None of these produce an error. All of them produce silence. A heartbeat monitor turns silence into an alert.

## Heartbeat vs Uptime Monitoring

|                    | Uptime monitor                      | Heartbeat monitor                          |
|--------------------|-------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Direction          | Monitor calls your service          | Your job calls the monitor                 |
| Watches            | APIs, websites, TCP/DNS endpoints   | Cron jobs, backups, workers, pipelines     |
| Detects            | Errors, timeouts, slow responses    | Missed runs, hangs, reported failures      |
| Needs public access | Yes (or a private location)        | No - only outbound HTTPS from the job      |
| Signal of failure  | A bad response                      | No response at all                         |

They're complements, not alternatives. Your API gets an uptime monitor; the job that sends invoices through that API gets a heartbeat. Because a heartbeat only needs an outbound request, it also works for jobs behind a firewall or on a laptop-sized box in a closet, where no external check could ever reach.

## Choosing a Period and Grace Period

These two numbers decide whether the monitor is useful or noisy.

**Period** is how often the job is supposed to run. An hourly cron job has a period of 1 hour. A nightly backup has a period of 1 day.

**Grace** is how much lateness you tolerate before alerting. It should cover:

- How long the job takes on a bad day, not an average one
- Start-up delay - scheduler jitter, cold containers, queue wait
- A small margin on top

Rules of thumb:

| Job                      | Typical runtime | Period  | Grace     |
|--------------------------|-----------------|---------|-----------|
| Cache warm-up            | 30 seconds      | 5 min   | 2-5 min   |
| Hourly data sync         | 5-15 min        | 1 hour  | 20-30 min |
| Nightly database backup  | 20-60 min       | 1 day   | 1-2 hours |
| Weekly report            | A few minutes   | 7 days  | 2-6 hours |

Too short a grace and you'll get paged every time the job runs slow, which trains everyone to ignore the alert. Too long and you lose the point - a nightly backup with a 3-day grace tells you it's broken after you needed it.

## Where to Put the Ping

**Ping at the end, on success.** A ping at the start proves the job launched, nothing more. The backup could have failed halfway. Pinging after the work completes means the heartbeat represents what you actually care about.

```bash
# crontab: ping only when the job succeeds
0 * * * * /path/to/job.sh && curl -fsS -m 10 --retry 3 https://api.openstatus.dev/heartbeat/<token>
```

A few habits that make pings reliable:

- **Set a timeout** (`-m 10`). A monitoring call should never be the thing that hangs your job.
- **Retry** (`--retry 3`). A single dropped request shouldn't open an incident.
- **Use `&&`, not `;`.** With `;` the ping fires even when the job failed, and the monitor reports green while nothing worked.

## Reporting Failures Explicitly

Waiting out the grace period works, but if your job knows it failed, it can say so right away. With openstatus, add `/fail` to the ping URL:

```bash
if /path/to/job.sh 2> /tmp/job.err; then
  curl -fsS -m 10 --retry 3 https://api.openstatus.dev/heartbeat/<token>
else
  curl -fsS -m 10 --retry 3 --data-binary @/tmp/job.err https://api.openstatus.dev/heartbeat/<token>/fail
fi
```

The monitor goes down within a minute instead of an hour later, and the error output you POST lands in the monitor's **Logs** tab next to the failed ping - no SSH-ing into the box to find out what happened. A job that keeps failing alerts once and stays down until it succeeds again, so a broken job doesn't spam your channel every run.

## What to Monitor With Heartbeats

Start with the jobs whose silent failure would hurt most:

- **Backups and database dumps.** The classic. A backup you haven't verified is a hope, not a backup.
- **Billing and invoicing runs.** Missed runs mean missed revenue or angry customers.
- **Data syncs and ETL pipelines.** Stale data in a dashboard is a quiet, expensive bug.
- **Certificate and token renewals.** These fail once a quarter and take production down when they do.
- **Cleanup and retention jobs.** Disks fill up slowly, then all at once.
- **Long-running queue workers.** Have them ping on a steady cadence so you know they're alive and consuming.

A useful test: for each scheduled job, ask "if this stopped running today, when would we find out?" If the answer is "when a customer complains" or "no idea", it needs a heartbeat.

## Common Mistakes

**Pinging at the start of the job.** You'll get a green monitor for a job that crashes every night after launch.

**One grace period for everything.** A 5-minute grace on a job that sometimes takes 40 minutes is an alert-fatigue generator. Size each one to its job.

**Sharing one ping URL across jobs.** If two jobs ping the same monitor, one can keep it green while the other is dead. One job, one monitor.

**Treating exit 0 as success.** A job that finds an empty input, processes nothing and exits cleanly still sends its ping, and the monitor stays green. A heartbeat only knows what the job tells it - if "did nothing" is a failure, have the job check its output and report `/fail`.

**Leaking the ping URL.** The token in the URL is the only credential. Treat it like a secret: keep it in your secret store, not in a public repo. If it leaks, regenerate it.

**Monitoring the job but not the alerts.** A heartbeat that alerts a channel nobody reads is just a log. Route it somewhere with an owner, same as any other [incident](/guides/what-is-incident-management).

## The Bottom Line

Uptime monitoring tells you when something stops answering. Heartbeat monitoring tells you when something stops *happening*. Scheduled jobs fail by going quiet, so they need a monitor that treats quiet as the alarm.

Ping at the end, on success. Size the grace period to the job's worst realistic runtime. Report failures explicitly when the job knows it failed. And give every job that matters its own heartbeat - the ones you forget about are exactly the ones that will stop.
