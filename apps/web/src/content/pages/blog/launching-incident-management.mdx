At the end of last year, we said we wanted to [move openstatus upstream](/blog/2026-roadmap).

Until now, we did two things: tell you when something broke (monitors) and tell your users about it (status pages). Everything in between happened in a messy Slack thread.

Today we're shipping incident management.

## Why?

Most incident tools (incident.io, Rootly, PagerDuty) are great, but they're built for enterprise teams with 500 engineers. You ask for a place to track an outage and you get a full-blown SaaS: 14 severity levels, sub-roles, Jira sync rules, and config screens nobody asked for.

We don't need all that. It's just Max and me. We wanted something small and opinionated that works for a 2-person startup the same way it works for a 30-person engineering team.

Before this, we were just using `#general`. And honestly, it sucked.

When an alert hits, say GCP queue depth spikes, dumping a message into `#general` and debugging inside a Slack thread falls apart fast:

- Slack's thread UX is awful when shit hits the fan. Half the replies are hidden behind *"43 more replies"*, someone pastes logs while someone else asks if we should update the status page, and nobody ticks *"Also send to channel."*
- Nobody knows if the issue is still happening, mitigated, or actually fixed.
- Two weeks later, when a customer or an auditor asks what happened on Tuesday at 10:14 AM, you're scrolling through `#general` trying to piece a timeline together.

## How it works

We kept the workflow as small as possible:

1. You declare an incident from Slack (or from a failing monitor in the dashboard, or via API / MCP).
2. openstatus creates a dedicated channel: `#inc-2026-10-14-gcp-queue`.
3. Everyone jumps in, debugging stays in one room, and the channel gets archived when you close the incident.

### I was wrong about slash commands

When we built our [Slack agent](/blog/openstatus-slack-agent) in February, I wrote:

> The only slash command I remember is the gif one.

I was half wrong. Natural language is great when you want the agent to draft prose for your status page. It's terrible when production is down and you just want to open a room in two seconds.

So we added `/openstatus incident`:

```bash
/openstatus incident declare GCP queue spike --sev critical
/openstatus incident note Draining stuck workers in europe-west1
/openstatus incident mitigate Queue depth dropping after worker restart
/openstatus incident resolve
/openstatus incident postmortem
```

You don't have to pass incident IDs inside the channel. We bind the Slack channel ID to the incident row, so the bot already knows where it is. You can also declare an incident straight from any Slack message's `⋯` menu (like when someone posts *"is the API timing out for anyone else?"*).

### Pinning messages to the timeline (📌 or right-click)

Nobody stops in the middle of debugging to open a dashboard and write timeline notes.

In `#inc-2026-10-14-gcp-queue`, if someone posts *"Edge config deploy at 09:38 changed EU routing, rolling back"*, you have two ways to save it:

1. **React with 📌 (`:pushpin:`)** on the message.
2. **Right-click the message** (or click `⋯`) and pick **Add to incident timeline** from the app shortcuts.

Either way, the bot replies with ✅ and appends the message to the incident timeline with its original timestamp, the author, and a permalink back to Slack.

### The edge cases we had to care about

The feature isn't the UI. It's the stuff that breaks when you actually rely on it during an outage:

- **What if Slack is down too?** We commit the incident to our database first, then open the Slack channel after. If Slack's API times out or fails to bind the channel, you don't lose the incident. If binding fails twice, the bot drops a "Link this channel" fallback button into the room.
- **Honest start times.** Nobody declares an incident at the exact second an outage starts. Usually you spend 10 minutes staring at logs first. If you declare an incident from a monitor's downtime row, we backdate `startedAt` to when the monitor actually started failing, not when you clicked the button.
- **Forgotten incidents.** We all fix the bug, go get coffee, and leave the incident open for a week. If an incident stays `open` or `mitigated` with no activity, the bot nudges the channel (after 1h for `critical`, 4h for `major`, 24h for `minor`, with exponential backoff so it doesn't spam you).

## Status pages are politics

From the incident channel, you can update your public status page using `@openstatus`.

Crucially: **declaring an incident does not touch your public status page.**

We wrote a whole post on why [status pages are politics](/blog/status-pages-is-politics). Status pages are 50% engineering and 50% politics 😂. Not every internal fire belongs on your public page. If a background queue lags for six minutes and no user notices, auto-publishing an outage just creates support tickets for nothing.

So we kept the two objects separate in the data model:

- **Incident (internal):** `open` → `mitigated` → `resolved` (or `canceled` for false alarms). Has a severity, a commander, internal notes, and a post-mortem.
- **Status report (public):** `investigating` → `identified` → `monitoring` → `resolved`. What customers actually read.

You can link them, and each keeps its own state. You can mark the incident `mitigated` internally while the public status page still says `monitoring`. Or you can never publish a status report at all.

When you *do* want to post publicly, just mention the bot in the incident channel:

```text
@openstatus update the status page: we identified a backlog in the EU job queue and are scaling workers now.
```

It posts an approval card in Slack. Nothing goes public until a human clicks **Approve**.

## Post-mortems without the LLM slop

When the incident is resolved, run `/openstatus incident postmortem`.

It pulls the incident metadata, the pinned timeline notes, the public status updates, and the Slack channel history (including thread replies, with bot messages stripped out), and generates a post-mortem draft.

One thing that annoyed me when testing this: LLMs love to invent corporate-sounding root causes when they don't have enough context. So we locked the prompt down. It outputs seven fixed sections, and if the channel transcript doesn't explain *why* something broke, it has to write that the root cause is unknown instead of guessing.

```markdown
## Summary
Between 10:14 UTC and 10:42 UTC, the GCP task queue experienced a spike in backlog depth, delaying webhook deliveries in EU.

## Impact
- **Duration:** 28 minutes (10:14 UTC – 10:42 UTC)
- **User impact:** Up to 12-minute delay on outgoing webhooks; 0 events dropped.

## Timeline
2026-10-14
- 10:14 UTC - Monitor alert triggered for `gcp-queue-depth-eu`
- 10:16 UTC - Incident declared by Thibault (major)
- 10:21 UTC - Identified stuck worker pool after v2.14.2 deploy
- 10:31 UTC - Rolled back worker image (mitigated)
- 10:42 UTC - Queue depth back to normal (resolved)

## Root cause
Connection leak in `v2.14.2` exhausted the worker Redis pool under burst load.

## What went well
- Alerting fired within 2 minutes.
- Dead-letter queue retries prevented dropped webhooks.

## What went wrong
- Staging tests didn't catch the connection pool limit.

## Action items
- [ ] Add Redis pool exhaustion alert (@thibault)
- [ ] Cap max concurrent jobs per worker (@max)
```

## Under the hood

We didn't build incident management as a separate silo. It's just another set of verbs in `@openstatus/services`, the same layer that backs our dashboard, API, [chat assistant](/blog/from-clickops-to-chatops), and [MCP server](/tooling/mcp-server).

That gives you two things for free:

1. **Every agent already knows how to use it.** If you connect Claude Code, OpenCode, or Cursor to our MCP server, `declare_incident`, `add_incident_note`, `set_incident_status`, and `draft_postmortem` are already there.
2. **Audit logs actually mean something.** Every mutation (whether you clicked a button in the dashboard, ran `/openstatus` in Slack, or let an agent draft a post-mortem) writes to the [audit log](/blog/audit-humans-and-agents) in the same database transaction, with the user who approved it. When an auditor asks for your incident trail, you don't have to fake it.
